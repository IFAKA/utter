01Why eight syllables

Few keys the sensor can tell apart. An AI does the rest.

Reading whole words from a closed mouth isn't reliable yet: the best published result still gets 42% of words wrong, and that is with the mouth moving and 19 hours of training data. A few very different tongue and jaw shapes are much easier: research reaches 88–97% on small sets like this one.

Each syllable uses a different part of the mouth, so each produces a different muscle pattern: k the back of the tongue, t the tip, l the sides, sh the whole tongue spread, ee the cheeks. The names themselves don't matter; we keep whichever eight the sensor separates best.

The AI turns those few keys into words. It reads the chat or the codebase and predicts what comes next, so most words need just one ya. The four-word reply in the demo takes seven syllables.

02The hardware

A 16-channel biosignal lab, the size of a matchbox.

Designed from the requirements up, not adapted from an EEG kit: the noise floor, sample rate and channel count all come from what silent-articulation EMG needs.

Front-end
2 × TI ADS1298, 24-bit, 16 channels
Sampling
2,000 samples/s per channel
Noise target
< 1 µVrms, 20–500 Hz (the signals are 5–50 µV)
Montage
Each electrode is recorded on its own. Pairs are formed in software, and the model picks the best sites.
Link
ESP32-S3 over Wi-Fi to your computer; recognition runs locally
Safety
Battery only: recording is blocked while USB is plugged in
Cost
≈ €100 per assembled board at prototype quantity
